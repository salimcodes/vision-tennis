The question: can a model tell flat, slice and kick serves apart? Play the three clips and ask the room to name each. Answer: A = flat, B = kick, C = slice. Why it matters: the serve starts every point, and knowing which serve a player picks (on break point, out wide) is scouting. Today that tagging is done by hand.
02 · Prediction

Shot type: easy.
Serve type: hard.

trained model > frontier models
What we expected, before running it: (1) Every model should tell a serve from a volley from a groundstroke, because the whole-body motion is very different. (2) Serve type should be hard, because flat, slice and kick differ mainly in racket angle and spin in a split second, and THETIS has no ball, so the spin is invisible. (3) A model trained on THETIS (X3D) should beat frontier models used zero-shot, since frontier vision models already struggle with fine spatial detail (e.g. telling whether two lines intersect). Data: THETIS (CVPR Workshops 2013): 12 stroke classes, amateur players, recorded with Kinect, no ball. Frontier condition: 60 RGB clips (5 per class, players p9, p18, p27, p36, p45, p55), each renamed to a random 8-character ID so the filename couldn't give away the label. Models: Claude Opus 5.5 (medium effort), GPT-6 (extra high), Gemini 4 Hydrogen. Trained condition: X3D video model trained on THETIS RGB (tested on 396 held-out clips), and the same X3D model on THETIS 3D skeleton data (252 test clips). Measured: exact stroke (12 classes, chance 8%), broad shot type (4 groups), and serve type (3 classes, chance 33%).
Results

Every model reads serves at chance.

X3D tested on its own held-out THETIS split (396 video / 252 skeleton clips); frontier models on 60 clips.
One sentence: models get better and worse at naming strokes (27% to 62%), but every single one, trained or not, gets serve type right about a third of the time, which is what guessing gets you. Numbers: all 12 strokes: X3D video 62%, X3D skeleton 52%, Claude 52%, GPT-6 37%, Gemini 27%. Serve type: X3D video 34/99, X3D skeleton 21/60, each frontier model 5/15. Broad shot type (frontier): Claude 80%, GPT-6 87%, Gemini 72%, so the big motion is seen and the fine detail is not.

Too few clips: 5 per class, so one video moves a class score by 20 points; 15 serves per frontier model. Different test sets: X3D was scored on its own held-out split (396 / 252 clips), the frontier models on 60 clips, so the comparison is indicative, not exact. The confound: THETIS was recorded without a ball. Spin, trajectory and bounce, which separate the serves, are missing. Chance-level serve accuracy may be a ceiling set by the data, not by the models. Models: broad shot type is largely solved (72–87% for frontier models), but the fine distinctions aren't, whether the model is trained on the task or not. Body motion alone may not carry the serve type. People: players and coaches judge a serve largely from the ball's flight and bounce. Take the ball away, as THETIS does, and the job gets much harder for anyone. Next: the Unity plan, synthetic serves with a ball and controlled spin, to test whether adding the ball is what's missing.
